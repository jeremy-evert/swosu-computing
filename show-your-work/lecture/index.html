Why show your work

  • Jobs. A repo is a work sample that does not need an interview.
  • Classmates. Someone stuck on your problem finds your answer.
  • Future you. You will not remember why you did it.
Notes · 3-8 min: Three reasons, one breath each. Then: next slide is what a stranger sees.

What a stranger sees in ten seconds

  1. What is this?
  2. Is it for me?
  3. Can I try it right now?
  4. Can I trust it?

Ask the room: what is one thing you look for when you open someone's repo?

Notes: Collect answers. Most will say README, screenshots, does it run. That is the whole lesson already.

Do well: nine habits, three groups

Explain it

  • One sentence: what, for whom, how to try
  • Say how you made it, AI included
  • Tell the truth about limits

Credit it

  • Pick a license on purpose
  • Credit everything you did not make
  • Say what it does with data

Test it as a stranger

  • Private window, phone, every link
  • Make trying it effortless
  • Branch and review before publishing
Notes · 8-15 min: Explain it, credit it, test it as a stranger. The full nine are on the guide; do not read them all aloud.

Avoid: five ways to get hurt

  • Secrets: passwords, keys, tokens. Public means copied.
  • Other people's data: names, emails, photos, classmates' work.
  • Your own paths: folder names, computer names, phone numbers.
  • Rights you do not have: books, images, worksheets, others' code.
  • Overclaiming: "secure", "accurate", "private" with no check.
Notes · 15-22 min: Five, not ten. The guide has the rest. Optional extras: facts about you (pet, birthday, maiden name) are also security-question answers; and hostnames in public files help an attacker who already has a foothold.

History keeps what you deleted

A secret you removed last week is still in the history.

This week we rewrote the history of a public repo of ours. It was a chosen cleanup: early commits carried names and paths that did not belong in public. We fixed it on purpose and said so.

Order if it ever happens to you: change the secret first, then clean up, then tell anyone affected. Do not hide it.

Notes: State plainly: a chosen cleanup, not an accident to hide. Old pull-request references can still hold old commits, so a rewrite is not a perfect erase. That is why prevention beats cleanup.

Live demo: run the checklist on one public repo

Pick five boxes. Tick or fail each one from evidence.

The checklist The repo

  1. Does the README say what it is in the first lines?
  2. Is there a LICENSE file in the top folder?
  3. Is there a credits list for what the author did not make?
  4. Is there a "how this was made" note, AI named?
  5. Do the links open from a private window?
Notes · 22-30 min: Students call out the answers. A tick needs something you can point at on screen. "Not sure" counts as a no. Optional: automate the link check with a script instead of clicking every link, and plant a harmless fake secret so you know your secret scan can actually find one.

AI and honesty: say how it was made

A "how this was made" note says: which tools, including AI; what you asked them to do; what you checked yourself.

A real one

Notes · 30-38 min: Show the real note. Hiding AI help is on the avoid list; naming it is a strength. A documented process also shows others how to repeat it.

The evidence ladder

  1. One AI says it.
  2. A second AI, in a fresh chat, agrees.
  3. You opened a source and read it.
  4. You ran or tested it yourself.
  5. A qualified person confirmed it.

Climb as high as the stakes demand. A typo can sit at rung 1. A license or private information needs rung 3 or higher.

Notes: Weakest at top, strongest at bottom. Ask: where would you put a claim like "this is secure"?

Use AI as a skeptical reader

Which claims in this README are not backed by something I did or checked? Which words (secure, private, accurate, safe, approved, tested) need evidence?

A good answer

Lists the specific words, says what evidence would justify each, and says what it could not check.

A bad answer

"Looks great! Very secure." No evidence asked for, no limits stated, nothing to verify.

Never paste a secret or private information into an AI chat.

Notes: Prompt 3 of 5 on the guide (https://jeremy-evert.github.io/swosu-computing/show-your-work/#prompts). Every flag is a to-do you verify yourself. AI is a good skeptic and a poor judge.

Write down what you decided

I chose ___.
I gave up ___.
I would change my mind if ___.

Three lines, no AI. A decision you cannot explain next month was a coin flip.

Notes: Example from the guide: "Publish only one example repo. Gave up showing the full workspace. Would change if every private file passes the same checklist."
