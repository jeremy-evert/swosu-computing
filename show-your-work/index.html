Do this well

  1. Say what it is in one sentence, who it is for, and how to try it.
  2. Make trying it effortless. A live link beats install steps.
  3. Pick a license on purpose and put the file in the top folder.
  4. Credit everything you did not make: libraries, fonts, sounds, images, worksheets, text.
  5. Say how you made it, including AI tools: what you asked, what you checked yourself.
  6. Say what it does with data, even if the answer is "nothing."
  7. Tell the truth about its limits: what is untested, unreviewed, or unfinished.
  8. Look at it fresh, in a private window and on a phone. Click every link.
  9. Make it easy to undo. Use a branch, get a review, then publish.

Avoid this

  1. Secrets: passwords, keys, tokens, login files. Public means copied.
  2. Other people's private information: names, emails, photos, grades, classmates' work.
  3. Your own private information: folder paths, computer names, phone numbers, photo locations.
  4. Material you have no right to share: books, images, worksheets, other people's code.
  5. Publishing your whole working folder, drafts and scratch files included.
  6. No license, or the wrong one.
  7. Overclaiming: "secure," "accurate," "private," "approved" without checking.
  8. Hiding AI help, or shipping what you did not read and run.
  9. Broken links, especially to private pages or paths on your own computer.
  10. Going silent. Say if it is finished, paused, or open to suggestions.

The process

These projects were not made in one pass. Each step left a record you can read.

  1. Write the plan first. What you are making, for whom, and what "done" means.
  2. Work in small steps on a branch. Each step has a written request and a written report.
  3. Check as you go. Tests, a stranger's eyes, and honest notes on what is not finished.
  4. Review before it goes public, with this page's checklist.
  5. Publish, then test the live version the way a stranger would.

See it in real repositories: the prompts that built Sorting and Searching, and the plan for this page.

Choosing a license

Pick one on purpose and say which. If you are unsure, ask your instructor. This is guidance for coursework, not legal advice.

What you madeA common choiceIn one line
CodeMIT or BSD 2-ClauseAnyone may use and change it if they keep your notice. The two are very close.
Writing, images, handouts, artCreative Commons CC BY 4.0Anyone may share and adapt it if they credit you.
Work you want to keep control ofNo license, with a clear noteOthers may look but may not reuse it.

A license covers only what you own. It does not cover a worksheet, picture, or text you copied from somewhere else.
