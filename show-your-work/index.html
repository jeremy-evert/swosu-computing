Your turn

Ten steps from "pick one small thing" to "know how to take it down," with what AI can help with and what a person must check.

Do the sameUse the checklist

Do this well

  1. Say what it is in one sentence, who it is for, and how to try it.
  2. Make trying it effortless. A live link beats install steps.
  3. Pick a license on purpose and put the file in the top folder.
  4. Credit everything you did not make: libraries, fonts, sounds, images, worksheets, text.
  5. Say how you made it, including AI tools: what you asked, what you checked yourself.
  6. Say what it does with data, even if the answer is "nothing."
  7. Tell the truth about its limits: what is untested, unreviewed, or unfinished.
  8. Look at it fresh, in a private window and on a phone. Click every link.
  9. Make it easy to undo. Use a branch, get a review, then publish.

Avoid this

  1. Secrets: passwords, keys, tokens, login files. Public means copied.
  2. Other people's private information: names, emails, photos, grades, classmates' work.
  3. Your own private information: folder paths, computer names, phone numbers, photo locations.
  4. Material you have no right to share: books, images, worksheets, other people's code.
  5. Publishing your whole working folder, drafts and scratch files included.
  6. No license, or the wrong one.
  7. Overclaiming: "secure," "accurate," "private," "approved" without checking.
  8. Hiding AI help, or shipping what you did not read and run.
  9. Broken links, especially to private pages or paths on your own computer.
  10. Going silent. Say if it is finished, paused, or open to suggestions.

Let an AI review it. Then you check.

An AI is a good skeptical reader and a poor judge. It can find what you missed, and it can invent problems or miss real ones. Treat every flag as a to-do item that you verify yourself. These prompts come from the Deciding with AI field guide (PDF), adapted for publishing.

Never paste a password, key, token, or someone's private information into an AI chat. Search for those on your own computer. Give the AI only text you would be fine seeing public.

Prompts

1 · Read it as a stranger
Here is my README. Read it as someone who has never seen this project. In three sentences say what it is, who it is for, and how to try it. Then list everything you could not tell.

[paste README]
2 · Hunt for things that should not be public
Here is the list of files I plan to publish and the text of my README. Which items might be private, risky, or not mine to share: names, emails, phone numbers, folder paths, computer names, locations, other people's work? For each, say why. Tell me what you could not check. I will verify every item myself.

[paste file list and README, with any secrets already removed]
3 · Catch the overclaims
Which claims in this README are not backed by something I did or checked? Which words (secure, private, accurate, safe, approved, tested) need evidence? For each, tell me what evidence would justify keeping it.

[paste README]
4 · Credit and license check
List every kind of material this project might contain that I did not make: code, fonts, images, sounds, text, worksheets, data. For each kind, tell me where to look to confirm its license. Do not tell me what the license is; I will read it myself.

[describe the project in a few lines]
5 · Premortem
It is three months from now and publishing this went badly. Give three likely reasons and an early warning sign for each. Then tell me what I could check today to prevent each one.

[describe the project and what you plan to publish]

Then check, from weakest to strongest

  1. One AI says it.
  2. A second AI, in a fresh chat, agrees.
  3. You opened a source and read it.
  4. You ran or tested it yourself.
  5. A qualified person confirmed it.

Climb as high as the stakes demand. A typo in a README can sit at rung 1. A license or someone's private information should reach rung 3 or higher.

Write down what you decided

Three-line decision log (write it yourself, no AI)
I chose ___.
I gave up ___.
I would change my mind if ___.

Put your answers in your project's notes. A decision you cannot explain next month was a coin flip.

The process

What comes after making a working resource? See our Open Source Education Roadmap for the planned next step: contributing to OER Commons, OpenStax hubs, Oklahoma OER programs, The Carpentries, and educational game platforms. These submissions are plans for after the semester, not completed publications.

These projects were not made in one pass. Each step left a record you can read.

  1. Write the plan first. What you are making, for whom, and what "done" means.
  2. Work in small steps on a branch. Each step has a written request and a written report.
  3. Check as you go. Tests, a stranger's eyes, and honest notes on what is not finished.
  4. Review before it goes public, with this page's checklist.
  5. Publish, then test the live version the way a stranger would.

Choosing a license

Pick one on purpose and say which. If you are unsure, ask someone who knows. This is general guidance, not legal advice.

What you madeA common choiceIn one line
CodeMIT or BSD 2-ClauseAnyone may use and change it if they keep your notice. The two are very close.
Writing, images, handouts, artCreative Commons CC BY 4.0Anyone may share and adapt it if they credit you.
Work you want to keep control ofNo license, with a clear noteOthers may look but may not reuse it.

A license covers only what you own. It does not cover a worksheet, picture, or text you copied from somewhere else.
