Escape the LLM Dungeon

Build playable learning projects with AI assistants: keep your judgment, protect people and data, and say plainly what you tested.

We can ask an AI to write a website, create a voice, summarize research, and invent a quiz in minutes. Making those things work together reliably, while protecting data and telling the truth about what we tested, is the real adventure.

This is a map for building useful, human-reviewed projects with AI assistants. You do not have to build a spelling game. WordWild is the example that shows us what went right and wrong.
WORKING DRAFT Not a completed or approved course. The eighteen checkpoints are an outline with exercises and expected evidence. Full tutorials, human curriculum review, accessibility review, rights clearance and learner testing are still future work. Connecting Gmail is optional; mock data is the default.

The destination

What will you have at the end?

A small, real website

HTML, CSS, JavaScript, accessible controls, audio if useful, and an address another person can open.

A trail of evidence

Clear prompts, a source and rights ledger, bug reports, tested user actions, known limitations and an honest release record.

The eighteen checkpoints are grouped into six stages. Open any chapter to see its practice task and the artifact it would produce. You may substitute fictional example data instead of connecting a real account.

Two real engineering lessons

Where WordWild earned its scars

The button that passed the tests

Enter submitted a spelling answer. Clicking Check it did nothing. The button had conflicting HTML types, and the automation mostly used Enter. One real-user action exposed what the green test dashboard missed.

Lesson: test the thing the person actually does.

The voice that got left behind

The original Flashcards had good contextual narration. The newer game copied some audio but omitted the first-screen sentence feature. Reusing the working audio and rendering missing Kokoro clips was more valuable than another endless redesign.

Lesson: protect working versions and verify all dependencies.

These are engineering examples, not evidence that the game improves spelling. Read the publicly documented limitations.

Safety doesn't require killing curiosity

Before you connect, code or publish

  • Permissions: use fictional inbox messages first; no student records, private mail or institutional data in an unapproved service.
  • Secrets: never place API keys, passwords or OAuth tokens in JavaScript, prompts, screenshots or Git history.
  • Research: distinguish verified source facts, memory tricks, legal/institutional rules and claims needing human review.
  • Accessibility: keyboard, mouse and touch all matter; provide transcripts and Stop/Mute for optional speech.
  • Licenses: know which parts are yours to publish, which are borrowed, and what the destination requires.
  • Tests: a passing suite is a record of actions tested, not a guarantee of safety, usability or learning.

Time-sensitive detail: OpenAI's gpt-4o-mini-tts Speech API model is deprecated and scheduled to shut down January 6, 2027. Our chapter compares TTS approaches and migration planning rather than making a deprecated model a permanent prerequisite. Current official voice documentation.

For instructors and future contributors

A teacher-ready package, in progress

The Open Education Roadmap calls for a lesson with objectives, worked examples, meaningful practice, formative assessment, accessible alternatives, rights and limitations. This field guide is our first public working draft of that package.

We've drafted an eighteen-chapter map and a single sample 45-minute workshop with two worked examples, six formative questions, and a provisional answer key. We have not taught or independently reviewed all eighteen lessons or uploaded them to OER Commons, OpenStax, Kolibri or the Carpentries.
