Where our teaching materials go next

Open Source Education Planning: a plan for contributing lessons and learning games to established educational communities.

We've learned to build and publish educational software on our own website. The next challenge is different: how do we contribute useful resources where other teachers can discover, adapt, review, and improve them?

Make something worth teaching. Verify it. Share it where educators already work. Keep improving it with the community.

This page is the plan, not a claim of completion. Our first outside-platform submissions are not started. I plan to work through these routes after the semester. Students can explore the roadmap now and see how the project develops over time.

Why this matters

Publishing something yourself is only the beginning.

There are different ways to share educational work, and they are not equivalent:

We can do this now

Host it ourselves

Our own GitHub Pages site hosts the game or lesson. It's public, but teachers still need to find it.

To explore

Publish or catalog it elsewhere

A platform such as OER Commons can host the educator-facing lesson or list a resource that lives on our site. These are two different submission routes.

To explore

Distribute or contribute upstream

Kolibri can distribute a packaged game. A community such as The Carpentries may review and integrate a lesson contribution into its maintained project.

That last distinction matters. A link in a catalog is not the same as a publisher hosting the game, and a submission is not the same as an accepted contribution.

The sequence

Our plan for after the semester

1

Start with a real learning problem

Choose one small, complete lesson or learning game. The first candidate is an educator guide accompanying WordWild, our public spelling-game example. Future projects may focus on third-grade mathematics or introductory computing.

2

Prepare a teacher-ready package

Set out objectives, a manageable lesson, worked examples, formative questions, answer explanations, accessibility guidance, rights and attribution, and known limitations. Ask another educator to review instructional claims.

3

Publish our first resource through OER Commons

Try Open Author to place a lesson on another organization's platform. Separately, consider Submit from Web to request a catalog listing for an existing public game. Record the real URL and review status.

4

Package a playable game for Kolibri

Make a self-contained HTML5 ZIP, with its own index page and relative assets. Upload through Kolibri Studio, test narration and controls, publish a channel if permitted, then confirm a separate Kolibri installation can import and play it.

5

Find a wider community to contribute to

Explore Oklahoma's OER ecosystem, college textbook support hubs, and open lesson communities. Learn the host's licensing, submission and AI-assistance policies; contribute something maintainers actually need.

6

Document what happened

Keep the submission receipts, URLs, review feedback, corrections, version history and unanswered questions. Update this roadmap with actual outcomes, including rejections or surprises.

The destinations

External ecosystems: where we could contribute

These are prospective routes, not organizations that have agreed to accept our work. Each has a different audience and publication model.

Higher ed · early priority

OpenStax + OER Commons hubs

Prepare an original, chapter-aligned activity that another college instructor can use. Find a specific textbook, open Instructor Resources, follow its Visit the Hub link when available, join the OER Commons group, and submit supplemental material.

First candidate: introductory computing topics such as binary representation or a misconception-driven worked example. Community posting is not OpenStax endorsement; check each book's license and AI-use conditions.

Computing lessons · human-led

The Carpentries Incubator

Find an existing lesson and its contribution guide, read recent issues, and choose one useful improvement. Have a human contributor review the educational need, write their own issue or PR description, and collaborate with maintainers.

Their May 2026 AI policy requires disclosure and bars agent-automated contributions. An Incubator lesson is not automatically an officially endorsed Carpentries lesson.

Interactive game distribution

Kolibri Studio

Package a complete offline-capable HTML5 game, upload it to a content channel, preview, publish and verify import in Kolibri. It can move the game's files into another educational distribution system.

A shared channel is not necessarily a public browser URL. We must test ZIP paths, audio, embedded browser behavior and rights.

The deliverable

What a complete contribution should contain

Our first submission will be deliberately small and complete, not an enormous unreviewed curriculum dump.

  • Who it serves: grade level, prerequisites, learning objectives and estimated time.
  • What to teach: a 20–45 minute lesson, two original worked examples and misconceptions addressed.
  • What the learner does: an interactive or offline activity, meaningful help and room to retry.
  • How an instructor checks understanding: formative items, explanations and an answer key.
  • How others can reuse it: accessible formats, source and asset credits, explicit rights/license, version and date.
  • What we actually know: test evidence, unresolved instructional claims, platform limits and a way to report corrections.
Example: WordWild has playable Flashcards and Printer games on our existing public site. The next proposed contribution is a reviewed teacher-facing spelling activity that links to the game. It is a candidate resource, not a proven literacy intervention.

A completed test is not proof that children learned more; a learner's thumbs-up is not a learning outcome; and an AI-generated answer is not an educator's verification. We will name what was tested and what still needs a person.

For students

You can study this process before we publish anything.

Choose one destination above. Ask: Who would benefit from the resource? Who hosts it? What rights does the author need? Who reviews it? What evidence would prove a contribution was accepted? Then compare its process to our Show Your Work checklist.

Try an existing WordWild game and look for one concrete improvement, whether that's a clearer clue, a mouse button, a sound control, a spelling explanation, or accessibility. A precise, reproduced problem is more valuable than a vague request for AI to “make it better.”

After the semester, I intend to work through this roadmap and share what really happened, including which outside organizations accepted work, what they asked us to change, and what did not work.

The destination isn't another repository with our name on it. It's useful teaching material that someone else can find, adapt, improve and use with learners we may never meet.
